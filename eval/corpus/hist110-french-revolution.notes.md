# Long-term causes

Prof. Lindqvist argues that the fiscal crisis was the trigger but not the cause of the Revolution. The deeper causes were structural: a society divided into three estates, Enlightenment ideas that questioned divine-right monarchy, and a rising bourgeoisie that had wealth but no political power.

The First Estate (clergy) and Second Estate (nobility) together made up about 2 percent of the population but were largely exempt from the main direct tax, the taille. The Third Estate, everyone else, carried the tax burden. Peasants also owed seigneurial dues to their lords and the tithe to the Church.

France's debt exploded after the Seven Years' War and especially after funding the American Revolution. By 1788 roughly half of royal spending went to paying interest on the debt. Finance ministers Turgot, Necker and Calonne all proposed taxing the privileged orders and all failed. A bad harvest in 1788 doubled bread prices in Paris, so urban workers were spending most of their wages on bread.

# 1789: Estates-General and National Assembly

Louis XVI called the Estates-General in May 1789 for the first time since 1614. The fight was over voting: by order (one vote per estate, so the privileged estates could outvote the Third two to one) or by head. On June 17 the Third Estate declared itself the National Assembly. Locked out of their usual hall, the deputies met at an indoor tennis court and swore the Tennis Court Oath on June 20, 1789, promising not to separate until France had a constitution.

On July 14, 1789 Parisian crowds stormed the Bastille, mainly to seize gunpowder. Only seven prisoners were inside. In the countryside the Great Fear spread in late July as peasants attacked manor houses and burned records of feudal dues. On the night of August 4 the Assembly abolished feudal privileges.

# Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen

Adopted on August 26, 1789. It declared that men are born and remain free and equal in rights, that sovereignty resides in the nation, and that law is the expression of the general will. It protected property as a sacred right. Prof. Lindqvist stressed that it said nothing about women or slaves; Olympe de Gouges answered it in 1791 with the Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen.

In October 1789 thousands of Parisian market women marched to Versailles over bread prices and forced the royal family to move to Paris (the Women's March on Versailles).

# Church and money

To deal with the debt, the Assembly nationalized Church lands in November 1789 and issued paper notes called assignats backed by those lands. Too many were printed, and they lost most of their value by 1795. The Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790) made priests state employees and required an oath of loyalty; about half refused, which split Catholic France and fed counter-revolution in regions like the Vendée.

# Radical phase and the Terror

War with Austria began in April 1792. After the attack on the Tuileries in August 1792 the monarchy was suspended, and the National Convention declared France a republic in September 1792. Louis XVI was tried and executed by guillotine on January 21, 1793.

Facing foreign war and internal revolt, the Convention gave emergency power to the Committee of Public Safety, dominated by Maximilien Robespierre. The levée en masse of August 1793 conscripted unmarried men aged 18 to 25 and mobilized the whole nation for war. The Law of Suspects of September 1793 allowed arrest of anyone suspected of opposing the Revolution. About 17,000 people were officially executed during the Terror, and many more died in prison or in the suppression of the Vendée.

Robespierre was overthrown and executed in July 1794, in the coup known as 9 Thermidor after the revolutionary calendar.

# Directory and Napoleon

The Directory (1795-1799) was a five-man executive that was weak and corrupt and relied on the army. Napoleon Bonaparte seized power in the coup of 18 Brumaire in November 1799, creating the Consulate. Prof. Lindqvist's view is that Napoleon both ended the Revolution and preserved its core gains, such as legal equality, in the Napoleonic Code of 1804.
